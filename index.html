Course description

Historical context and central themes.

Philosophers writing from the 17th century onwards were preoccupied with conflict and discontent in Europe. This led many to begin thinking and writing on forms of sovereignty other than that of absolute monarchy, the vestiges of Bodin’s reflections. We therefore have the emergence of the social contract tradition that challenged established ideas of monarchical rule. This also marked the beginning of liberal ideas and values in the study of the state and political authority.

Therefore, in Western Political Thought – II, students will be acquainted with the works and traditions of some dominant themes and ideas, i.e. political obligation, rights, utility, liberty, emancipation. Like in Part I of the Course, the context and contemporary relevance of these traditions remain a crucial aspect of study.

Course objectives

  • Understand the preoccupation of Western philosophers with political issues and political questions of their times.
  • Appreciate the close links that politics shared with ethics and moral values.
  • Situate contexts within which ideas of liberty and equality emerged.
  • Trace the intellectual roots of some contemporary ideologies.

Pedagogy

Lectures, discussions, reading excerpts from original works and documentaries/videos (where available).

CIA I · AI debate: Locke vs Rousseau

Brief, submission requirements and rubric.

AI Debate: Locke vs Rousseau

20 marks

This assignment is designed to help students apply Locke and Rousseau to contemporary issues, develop coherent arguments, engage critically with competing political ideas, reflect on AI-generated reasoning, and connect classical political thought to modern debates. Students will engage in a debate with an AI chatbot prompted to act as an ardent contemporary supporter of either Locke or Rousseau. Each student is assigned the opposing position and must defend it throughout the debate.

Submission
Submit a complete debate transcript containing at least 10 substantive exchanges.
Deadline in course plan: November 20

AI defends Locke

Student position: Rousseauian

Focus on popular sovereignty, the general will, civic freedom, political equality, the social origins of inequality, and the critique of private property.

AI defends Rousseau

Student position: Lockean

Focus on natural rights, individual liberty, government by consent, limited political authority, private property derived from labour, and the right to resist tyranny.

Bonus marks

Students may earn up to 2 bonus marks by correctly identifying mistakes, oversimplifications, or misinterpretations made by the AI and supporting corrections with course materials.

Full prompt 1: AI as a contemporary Lockean (click to expand)

You will act as an ardent contemporary follower of John Locke. I will argue from a Rousseauian perspective. Throughout this debate, defend and apply Locke’s ideas, especially: natural rights; individual liberty; government by consent; limited political authority; private property derived from labour; the right to resist tyranny. You are not John Locke himself. Instead, you are a contemporary supporter of Locke who applies Lockean ideas to both historical and contemporary issues. Challenge my arguments directly and defend Locke’s position. Do not simply explain Locke’s ideas. Debate them. Use simple, clear, and accessible language suitable for undergraduate students studying political theory. Avoid overly academic vocabulary, technical philosophical jargon, and unnecessarily long explanations. Keep most responses between one and three paragraphs. You may use contemporary examples, but make it clear when you are applying Locke’s ideas to modern issues rather than directly citing Locke. Do not become overly agreeable. If my argument is weak, challenge it. End most responses with a question, criticism, or counterargument that keeps the debate going. I will start the debate with a Rousseauian criticism of Locke: [Insert your criticism here.]For example: I object to your understanding of private property. From a Rousseauian perspective, private property is the main source of social inequality. Rousseau argues that the moment someone enclosed land and claimed it as their own, the foundations of inequality were created. Why should we regard private property as a natural right rather than as a source of domination and social division? (This is only an example; you are encouraged to use your own initial position for the debate.)
Use very simple, accessible, beginner-friendly language throughout the debate. Write as if you are speaking to first- or second-year undergraduate students taking an introductory course in political theory. Avoid technical philosophical jargon unless it is essential (for example, terms such as “general will” or “natural rights”). When you use specialized concepts, explain them briefly in plain language. Do not write like a textbook, journal article, or encyclopaedia. Avoid long paragraphs, complex sentence structures, and unnecessarily sophisticated vocabulary. Keep most responses between 100 and 150 words. Focus on one or two key arguments at a time rather than presenting many ideas at once. Use concrete examples whenever possible. If discussing contemporary issues, clearly explain how they relate to Locke’s or Rousseau’s ideas. Your goal is to have a genuine debate with a student, not to deliver a lecture.

Ungraded in-class activity

Close reading, annotating and summarising.

Activity description

Students engage in an in-class close-reading activity based on a text selected by the instructor and aligned with the syllabus. Each student receives a copy with wider margins for annotation. Students read critically, identify key ideas, and record observations in the margins. They then prepare a concise summary of the central argument, themes and significant details. Different students may receive different texts. This is an individual, in-class activity. The summary should be approximately 300 words. The activity carries no marks; the rubric is used for feedback.

CIA III · Longitudinal reflective assessment

Repeated political question and end-of-course reflection.

Assessment brief

20 marks
Deadline in course plan: February 17

Essay 1 — beginning of semester

5 marks · 500–800 words

When, if ever, are people justified in challenging the authority of the state? Reflect on this question in the context of protests against governments in India (recent or old) and elsewhere in the world. Critically reflect on how your social location and subjectivity (class or caste location, gender, community, political affiliation, age/life stage, broader personal/moral commitments, family background, etc.) may have shaped your perspective.

Rubric: Originality.

Essay 2 — towards the end of semester

5 marks · 500–800 words

When, if ever, are people justified in challenging the authority of the state?

Rubric: Originality.

Reflective Essay — towards the end of semester

10 marks · 500–800 words

How do you assess your own intellectual development during the course? Discuss how engagement with western political thought helped you reaffirm, revise, complicate, or abandon some earlier assumptions. Reflect not only on changes in conclusions but also on changes in how you think about political questions.

Rubric: Originality (5 marks); Critical Thinking (5 marks).

Important: independent thinking and AI policy
These assignments are intended to reward genuine thinking rather than polished prose. Students will not be penalised for imperfect writing, uncertainty, changing their minds, or making mistakes. Submissions wholly or substantially generated by AI will not be accepted; they will be returned and must be rewritten independently. An honest but imperfect response is valued more highly than a polished response that does not reflect the student’s own thinking.
